1. Strategic context

Fusion at the intersection of energy and power projection

Over the 2025–2045 horizon, fusion is increasingly discussed not just as a technological breakthrough, but as a potential sovereignty lever:

A possible long-term pillar of energy sovereignty, alongside renewables, fission and storage.
A driver of industrial sovereignty across materials, magnets, superconductors, advanced manufacturing and control systems.
A factor in defence and strategic autonomy, through secure power for bases, fleets, infrastructures or space systems in the very long term.
A foundation for compute and AI sovereignty, powering energy-intensive digital infrastructures (HPC, data centres, AI clusters).

Various countries, alliances and private actors are exploring fusion from different angles. Without referring to any specific project or roadmap, it is plausible that questions such as “Who controls fusion capacity, and under what doctrine?” will remain strategically sensitive.

The phrase “Fusion Sovereignty” is one intuitive way of naming this cluster of concerns. FusionSovereignty.com is a straightforward, C-suite-readable .com for that doctrine.

2. A descriptive view of “Fusion Sovereignty”

What the banner can cover (without prescribing policy)

In a descriptive, non-normative sense, a Fusion Sovereignty doctrine can be seen as a way to organise thinking around four broad axes:

Energy sovereignty: the potential role of fusion in long-term, low-carbon and secure energy supply.
Industrial sovereignty: control over critical components, supply chains and know-how needed for fusion ecosystems.
Defence & strategic autonomy: implications of fusion for military basing, logistics and strategic infrastructures in the long run.
Digital & compute sovereignty: interactions between fusion-powered generation and high-density compute / AI infrastructure.

FusionSovereignty.com does not dictate how these axes should be prioritised or governed. It provides a name-space under which governments, alliances, think tanks or private actors can build their own frameworks, scorecards, dialogues or observatories.
